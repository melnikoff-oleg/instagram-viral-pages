## What the video is doing

- **A/B rhythm.** Static hero portrait → vehicle at full speed → portrait → vehicle → vehicle → portrait → portrait. The still shots give the fast shots contrast; the fast shots give the stills a reason to exist. Nothing is "medium" speed.
- **Every still shot has secondary motion.** Cape in wind (1, 7), bats (6), city lights. The subject is still, the frame is not.
- **Every vehicle shot is one simple vector.** Toward the camera (2), left→right (4), away from the camera (5). Camera does exactly one thing per clip: retreat, track alongside, follow. No arcs, no combined moves.
- **Speed is shown by the background, not the subject.** In 4 and 5 the vehicle barely moves inside the frame; the smeared city and streaking tunnel lights carry the speed. That is the trick that makes it look "very fast" without the subject leaving the frame.
- **Tunnel and elevated-road settings** give repeating structures (arches, lamps, pillars) that streak and read as speed instantly.
- **Realism:** heavy motion blur on backgrounds, tyre smoke and dust that stay on the ground, warm practical tunnel lights with flare, lifted blacks, grain. No visible morphing; the Tumbler keeps its silhouette through the whole pass.
- **Hook:** frame 0 is a readable Batman silhouette against a twilight skyline. Instantly legible, cape already moving.

## Lessons for cars

- Shot 4 (side profile, matched tracking, smeared background) and shot 5 (follow from behind, lights streaming) are the two highest-energy shots and the two simplest to describe. Both are car shots in all but name.
- Shot 2 (vehicle grows toward a retreating camera) is the strongest opener-style shot: the subject gets bigger and the lights streak at once.
- The still portraits only work because the hero is a person with a cape. Our hero is a car: a still car has no cape. This is why our rule is "the car never stands still".
